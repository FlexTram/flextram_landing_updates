Buying venue transportation equipment before year-end. What the tax rules say.

Equipment that's ready to run by the end of your tax year can come off this year's return. Here's what Section 179, the permanent 100% first-year deduction, and the placed-in-service rule mean for venues, and why most of it doesn't apply to public ones.

$2,560,000.

That's how much equipment a business can deduct in a single year under Section 179 for tax years beginning in 2026, according to IRS Publication 946. The limit only starts shrinking once a company puts more than $4,090,000 of qualifying property into service in the year.

A venue's guest transportation fleet costs a small fraction of that. So for most private operators, the question in late November is a simple one. Does the purchase land on this year's return or next year's?

One note before going further. We build trams. We don't prepare tax returns. Everything below comes from IRS publications, and your accountant should have the last word on your situation.

Can a venue deduct transportation equipment in the year it buys it?

Often, yes, if the buyer is a taxable business. Two rules do most of the work.

Section 179 lets a business deduct the cost of qualifying equipment up front instead of spreading it over several years. The property has to be acquired by purchase, which rules out gifts, inheritances, and purchases from related parties. If it's used for both business and personal purposes, business use has to be more than 50%. The deduction is also limited to your taxable income from actively running a business that year. Any amount over that limit carries forward to the next year.

Bonus depreciation covers much of the same ground without the dollar cap or the income limit. In January the IRS issued guidance on the permanent 100% first-year depreciation deduction for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. Publication 946 says qualified property includes tangible property depreciated over 20 years or less, and that it can be new or, in certain cases, used. Our October article on the 2026 tax window walks through both rules in more detail, including financing and recapture.

Here's a simple illustration. A corporation with enough taxable income to absorb it buys $100,000 of qualifying equipment and deducts all of it this year. Corporations pay a flat 21% federal rate, so that deduction lowers this year's federal tax by $21,000. Under regular depreciation the same $100,000 is still deductible, but only part of it this year and the rest over the years that follow. Partnerships, S corporations, and LLCs taxed like them pass the deduction through to their owners, who pay at their own rates. State rules differ too.

Tow vehicles are a separate conversation. Vehicles carry their own rules. One example: Section 179 on a heavy sport utility vehicle is capped at $32,000 for tax years beginning in 2026. Ask your accountant how the tow vehicle is treated apart from the passenger units.

What does "placed in service" mean, and why does December 31 matter?

A signed purchase order isn't enough. The IRS says property is placed in service when it is ready and available for a specific use.

Two things follow from that.

First, the equipment has to be delivered and ready before your tax year ends. For calendar-year companies, that's December 31. If your fiscal year ends in a different month, your date is different.

Second, delivery alone doesn't settle it. The IRS's example is a machine delivered in one year but not installed and working until the next. That machine is placed in service in the second year. The same example goes on to say that if the machine had been ready for use when it arrived, it would count in the first year, even if nobody used it until the next.

So a venue whose season opens in February can take delivery in December and still count the equipment this year. It has to be complete and ready to run when the year ends. A unit sitting on a truck or waiting on parts doesn't qualify.

Is the deduction going away in 2027?

No. And you should be skeptical of anyone who implies it is.

The Section 179 limit is adjusted for inflation each year, and the 100% first-year deduction is now permanent. There's no cliff on January 1.

What December 31 decides is timing. Equipment ready this year can come off this year's taxable income. Equipment that shows up in January waits a full year for the same treatment. If 2026 was a strong year, that timing is worth real money. If it wasn't, waiting may cost you nothing.

What if the venue is a university, a city, or a nonprofit?

Then most of this doesn't apply. An organization that pays no income tax has nothing to deduct against. Publication 946 also lists property used by certain tax-exempt organizations and by governmental units among the property that generally doesn't qualify for Section 179, with narrow exceptions.

Plenty of stadiums and campuses fall in that group. For them, year-end matters for different reasons:

  • Budget. Capital money that goes unspent in one budget year doesn't always come back in the next.
  • The calendar. Spring festivals, graduation, and the first home dates arrive quickly after the holidays. Equipment ordered now is on site and staff are trained before the first crowd.
  • Who buys. Many public venues hand guest transportation to a private parking or hospitality operator. That operator may be the one buying the equipment, and the tax rules above may apply to them. How the arrangement is written matters here. Equipment leased to a government or tax-exempt body can be treated differently from equipment an operator owns and runs itself, so this is one to take to an accountant before signing.

When does owning beat renting?

Renting fits a festival that runs one weekend a year. The equipment arrives, works the event, and leaves.

A venue with a full calendar is in a different spot. An arena with dozens of event nights, a stadium with a home schedule plus concerts, or a campus that moves people every week will pay for the same rental again and again. At some point the rental line costs more than the equipment.
